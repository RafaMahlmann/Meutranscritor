Before you record: who has to agree

If you record a conversation with other people, the law may ask you to tell them or to get their consent first. In the United States the answer depends on the state. This page shows what the laws say, with a link to each one.

This is not legal advice. It is a guide to the texts of the laws, read on November 11, 2026. The person who records is the one responsible for following them.

The short answer

Federal law

One participant is enough

Under federal law it is not unlawful to record "where such person is a party to the communication or where one of the parties to the communication has given prior consent".

18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d)

State law

Some states ask for everyone

Federal law is the minimum. States "may impose stricter rules", and about a dozen do, for calls, for conversations in person, or for both.

Reporters Committee recording guide

The habit that covers both

Ask, on the recording

Start recording and say: "This conversation is being recorded and transcribed. Do you agree?" A clear yes at the start is the simplest proof you can have.

Four things the table teaches

Secret is the problem

Several of these laws are written around recording secretly or with a hidden device. When everyone in the room knows a recording is running, the picture changes in states like Massachusetts and Montana.

Private is the scope

California, Illinois and Washington speak of confidential or private conversations. A public meeting is a different case.

Calls and rooms can differ

Connecticut, Nevada and Oregon have one rule for phone calls and another for conversations in person.

An announcement can be consent

Washington's law says consent counts as given when one party announces the recording to everyone and that announcement is recorded. That is the idea behind asking on the recording.

What Vox can and cannot do

  • Vox cannot know where you are, who is in the room, or what you agreed with them. Getting consent is up to the person who records.
  • Vox does not help anyone record people in secret.
  • If you record conversations for work, make asking part of the first ten seconds. It protects the people you talk to, and it protects you.

Questions people ask

Is this legal advice?
No. It is a summary of what the laws say, with links so you can read them. Laws change, courts read them in different ways, and your situation may have details this page does not cover. For a real decision, ask a lawyer where you live.
I only record myself thinking out loud. Do I need anyone's consent?
If you are the only person speaking, there is nobody else in the recording to ask.
Is telling people enough, or do they have to say yes?
It depends on the state. Some laws are about people knowing (Montana, Oregon in person, and Massachusetts, which is about secret recording). Others say consent. Asking out loud and getting a clear yes at the start of the recording covers both.
What if the people on a call are in different states?
The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press tells journalists to "err on the side of caution and assume that the stricter state law will apply". In practice that means asking everyone.
I record patients or clients. Is consent to record all I need?
No. Health and professional rules add duties about how recordings are stored, shared and deleted, and this page does not cover them. See also how the speaker separation services treat your audio.
What about other countries?
This page covers the United States only. Other countries have their own rules about recording and about personal data.
